One year ago today, on January 27, 2010, I walked through the doors of Microsoft for the first time as an employee.

For years, Microsoft had been the company I hoped I might someday work for. Like many IT professionals, I had built solutions on Microsoft technologies, earned certifications, followed product releases, and imagined what it would be like to work alongside the people behind the products I used every day.

Now, one year later, I find myself looking back and reflecting on what I've learned.

Surprisingly, the most valuable lesson wasn't technical.

It wasn't about a particular product, customer, or deployment.

It was this:

> **Success at Microsoft isn't about having all the answers.**
>
> **It's about learning how to find them.**

## A Lot of Onboarding

My first stop was new-hire orientation at the Alpharetta office in Sanctuary Park. From there, I joined a few shadow engagements with Premier Field Engineers across the Southeast, rotated onto a Critical Situation—or "CritSit"—team in the Las Colinas office, and then spent several weeks at Microsoft Services University in the Seattle and Redmond area.

It was a lot of onboarding.

I had come from the consulting world in the Microsoft partner ecosystem, where I was accustomed to moving quickly from one customer engagement to the next. I was chomping at the bit to contribute, and I found the stretches of "bench time" surprisingly uncomfortable.

In hindsight, that slower start was doing something important. It was teaching me how Microsoft worked: not only its tools, systems, and acronyms, but the network of people and roles behind every successful customer engagement.

## The Myth of the Expert

Before joining Microsoft, I assumed the most successful people around me would be the ones who knew everything.

The technical experts.

The seasoned consultants.

The engineers who could immediately diagnose a problem and prescribe the perfect solution.

Then I started working with customers.

Almost immediately, I found myself involved in situations that stretched beyond my experience level: new technologies, complex enterprise environments, and problems that didn't have obvious answers. Rather than having everything figured out, I found myself reaching out to experienced engineers, specialists, and peers to learn faster and validate my thinking.

One exchange from late this year stuck with me:

```text
Office Communicator
November 2010

Mike:
Do we have logs uploaded yet?

████████ ████████:
I'm not too good on the debug or SQL side
of things, so not sure where I can help.

Mike:
Me either :(
```

*Engineer name redacted.*

At first glance, it seems insignificant.

What struck me was that experienced engineers were perfectly comfortable admitting what they didn't know.

As a new employee, that was surprisingly encouraging.

## Discovering How Microsoft Really Works

One of the biggest surprises of my first year has been how collaborative Microsoft is.

Before joining, I imagined expertise living primarily within individuals.

Inside the company, I've learned that expertise often exists within networks.

When difficult customer situations arise, specialists, Premier Field Engineers, Technical Account Managers, consultants, support teams, and customers work together to understand the issue and identify a path forward.

The answer rarely comes from one person.

The answer usually comes from many people working together.

### What I Thought Success Looked Like

```text
          Expert
             |
             |
          Customer
```

### What I Found at Microsoft

```text
     Specialist
          |
          |
TAM ---- Team ---- PFE
          |
          |
      Customer
```

The most effective people I've worked with aren't necessarily the people with all the answers.

They're the people who know how to connect expertise to problems.

## Learning to Ask Better Questions

At the beginning of the year, I thought growth came from accumulating answers.

Today, I think growth might come from learning how to ask better questions.

The more customer engagements I've participated in, the more I've realized that complex problems rarely have simple solutions.

One lesson I'm still learning is that asking for help isn't a sign of weakness.

If anything, it often speeds up learning.

Another conversation captured this perfectly:

```text
Mike:
I'll keep an eye on the thread,
but won't burn any hours on this one
unless I think I can help.
```

Earlier in the year, I probably would have tried to contribute to everything.

Over time, I've started learning that effectiveness isn't measured by participation.

It's measured by impact.

Sometimes the most valuable thing you can do is help connect the right people.

## Building a Network of People Smarter Than Me

One of the best decisions I made this year was reaching out whenever I encountered something unfamiliar.

Every customer engagement introduced me to someone new.

Every difficult issue exposed me to people with different experiences and expertise.

Every conversation expanded my understanding of both Microsoft and the technologies we support.

Looking back, the most valuable thing I've gained this year may not be technical knowledge.

It may be relationships.

The people I've met throughout Microsoft have consistently shared their knowledge, offered guidance, and taken time to help me learn. Many of the lessons I've learned this year came from conversations with people who had already faced the challenges I was encountering for the first time.

## 2010 in Numbers

Not every milestone can be measured, but a few stand out:

- First year at Microsoft
- Multiple enterprise customer engagements
- Collaboration with Premier Field Engineers, Technical Account Managers, specialists, and customer teams
- Dozens of new professional relationships
- Countless new products, technologies, and acronyms
- Far more questions asked than answers given

### Most Valuable Lesson

> Curiosity scales faster than expertise.

## What I'm Learning About

Some of the topics that occupied much of my attention this year included:

- SharePoint Online
- Enterprise collaboration technologies
- Online services
- Customer consulting
- Troubleshooting and investigations
- Working across technical and business teams
- Premier support delivery models

Many of the conversations that shaped my first year touched one or more of these areas.

## Resources That Shaped My First Year

Throughout the year, some of the most valuable resources weren't documents or technical articles.

They were people.

- Premier Field Engineers
- Technical Account Managers
- Customer support organizations
- Specialist communities
- Technical discussion groups
- Customer engagement teams

Repeatedly, I found that the fastest way to solve a problem was to connect with someone who had already solved a similar one.

## Looking Ahead

A year ago, Microsoft was a company I hoped I might one day join.

Today, it's a company filled with people who challenge me, teach me, and help me grow every day.

I still have much more to learn than I know.

There are technologies I haven't mastered, customers I haven't worked with, and experiences I haven't had yet.

But after my first year, I've come to appreciate something important:

Nobody succeeds here alone.

The more I learn, the more I realize that expertise isn't about having every answer.

It's about staying curious enough to ask the next question.

It's about building relationships.

It's about being willing to learn.

And if my first year has taught me anything, it's that those may be the most valuable skills of all.

> *"The answer rarely comes from one person. The answer usually comes from many people working together."*
>
> That's probably the most important thing I learned during my first year at Microsoft.
