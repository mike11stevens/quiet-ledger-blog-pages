Hi, I’m Mike Stevens.

I’m a husband, a father, and a lifelong technology enthusiast who has spent more than two decades working in IT. My career has carried me from building PCs and experimenting with code to consulting, enterprise services, cloud technology, learning, and technical training. Along the way, the work has become less about knowing every answer and more about asking better questions, connecting the right people, and helping others find their way through complexity.

## The early curiosity

My interest in technology started long before it became a career. Thanks to a curious, engineer-minded father, I grew up around computers in the 1980s and ’90s—building PCs, reading computer magazines, dialing into bulletin board systems, learning to code, and making crude HTML pages. I was fascinated by what technology could do and by the feeling that there was always another layer to uncover.

That curiosity eventually became a profession. I worked in consulting and the Microsoft partner ecosystem, earning certifications and helping customers turn products into working solutions. In 2010, I joined Microsoft—a place I had imagined working since I was young—and began a long chapter supporting enterprise customers, solving difficult problems, and learning from people far smarter than me.

## A career measured in chapters

Over the years, my roles evolved with the industry. I moved from consulting and support into cloud learning, Azure training, and work focused on helping people build the skills required by technology that never stands still. The tools, titles, and platforms changed. The durable parts were curiosity, humility, empathy, and the ability to create clarity when things felt complicated.

One of the most consequential chapters began in 2013, when my wife, Wendy, and I moved our family to Germany for an opportunity supporting the United States Air Forces in Europe. The move expanded my understanding of technology’s real purpose: systems and architecture matter, but only because they help people accomplish something larger. It also taught me that, once you are married with children, a career decision is never only a career decision. It is a family decision involving schools, friendships, community, stability, sacrifice, and trust.

## The life around the work

Marriage and parenthood have changed how I define ambition and success. Earlier in my career, I often measured progress through expertise, responsibility, and the next opportunity. Experience has made the questions broader: Am I continuing to grow? Am I helping other people grow? Am I present for the people I love? Is the work supporting a good life, or quietly consuming it?

I have not solved those questions once and for all. I have learned that boundaries matter, that asking for help is a strength, and that no professional achievement belongs to one person alone. My family has carried the weight of relocations, long days, career changes, and every experiment in becoming a slightly wiser version of myself. Whatever success I have had is part of our shared story.

## Still evolving

More than twenty years into an IT career, I am still a student. I remain interested in the intersection of technology, learning, organizational change, and the human experience of work. New tools—including ChatGPT and Codex—are now part of how I explore ideas, recover old writing, build projects, and test my assumptions. They do not replace judgment or perspective; they give me new ways to question, create, and revise.

Vampire Workday is where some of that thinking ends up. The name reflects a conviction I have earned over time: working for the weekend kind of sucks. A career is important, but it is only one part of a life that also includes marriage, parenthood, health, curiosity, mistakes, reinvention, and everything in between.
